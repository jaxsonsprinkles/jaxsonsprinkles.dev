You Can Just Email People

I sent one email asking for a chance. Three months later my code was live for thousands of students.

In March, I sent a cold email to the head of marketing at a startup called Appybara. I didn't know anyone there. I didn't have a warm intro. I just had a portfolio, a few shipped projects, and a sentence that said "I think I could help." I didn't expect much. Most cold emails go nowhere, and I'd sent enough of them by then to know that.

This one didn't go nowhere. The marketing lead liked what he saw and connected me with the founder, Aleksei. A few conversations later, I was a Full-Stack Software Engineering Intern at an AI-powered college application platform, the kind of tool that helps students write essays, get admissions feedback, and figure out how likely they are to get into the college of their dreams. I'd be reporting straight to the founder daily, on a small team, working on the real product.

I want to be clear about what that meant, because "internship" can mean a lot of things. It didn't mean waiting for Aleksei to tell me what to do every day or building a random feature in a PR that would never get merged. It meant that my code would go out to real students using the product to plan their futures. That's more pressure than I've ever felt.

Once I actually started, the thing I hadn't anticipated was how much of the job wasn't "add a feature" but "make the product trustworthy." Appybara tells highschoolers how strong their college application is and what to fix. If that advice is inconsistent, or the platform feels like it can be gamed, or the interface makes you doubt what you're looking at, the whole thing falls apart.

That paradigm is what connects almost everything I shipped. The admissions review tool scores a student's profile using an LLM, and I found that if you regenerated a review on the same profile, the scores would drift (sometimes by a lot) because the model had no memory of its own last answer. I fixed that by feeding previous scores back into the prompt as anchors, which took the standard deviation across repeated regenerations from 0.49 to 0.0. Same profile in, same answer out.

What surprised me most was the amount of independence that teamwork has. Not all of my assignments were supposed to be cherry-picked from Linear; I had to use the site, find issues, and submit them on my own. At Appybara, I had to justify "why does this matter" for every line of a PR, and it has made me a sharper engineer than any project I built on my own could have.

I still don't know exactly where this internship will lead me in the future. But I know it started with one email at 10:26 AM. The ask is usually cheaper than you think, and the work is usually more available than you think: you just have to be brave enough to go looking for it.
